1. Executive Summary
The KSP Crime Analytics and Visualization Platform is a proposed state-of-the-art system that replaces the
Karnataka State Police current Excel-based, siloed analytical ecosystem with an integrated, AI-driven intelligence
hub. The platform is purpose-built to address the four pain points identified in the Datathon challenge: data silos
and manual processes, the absence of advanced analytics, fragmented information flow to the State Crime Records
Bureau, and the reactive rather than proactive posture of policing. Rather than adding another reporting tool on top
of the existing mess, the platform consolidates crime records, offender data, victim data, modus operandi
taxonomy, socio-economic indicators, and financial transactions into a single analytical substrate, and exposes this
substrate through three complementary pipelines: an interactive visualization and spatial pipeline, a criminal
network link analysis pipeline, and a predictive and anomaly detection pipeline.
The platform is architected around three parallel analytics pipelines that share a common data layer and a common
explainability framework. The Visualization and Spatial Pipeline replaces static spreadsheets with interactive
dashboards and geospatial maps, enabling district-level drill-down, spatiotemporal hotspot detection, and
emerging-trend red-zone pulsing that flags spikes against historical averages. The Network Link Analysis
Pipeline replaces independent silos with a node-based visualization that connects suspects, victims, and recurring
locations, surfaces repeat offenders across jurisdictions, and detects hidden criminal associations through
community detection algorithms. The Predictive and Anomaly Pipeline moves the SCRB from reactive reporting
to a strategic intelligence hub by overlaying crime data with socio-economic indicators, deploying AI-driven risk
scoring on districts, and running anomaly detection that flags incidents deviating from standard behavioral patterns.
The system is designed ground-up for deployment on Zoho Catalyst, leveraging 18 native Catalyst services so that
no external infrastructure, GPU hardware, or expensive ML platform is required. The analytics logic runs on
Serverless Functions; the AI models are trained and served on Zia AutoML and QuickML; the relational crime
database lives on Catalyst Data Store; the network graph cache and audit log live on Catalyst NoSQL; the
interactive dashboards are hosted on Catalyst Slate; PDF intelligence briefings are rendered by SmartBrowz; nightly
hotspot and forecast refresh jobs run on Catalyst Cron; and red-zone alerts are pushed to field officers via Catalyst
Push Notifications triggered by Catalyst Signals. This Catalyst-native approach means the prototype has zero
external dependencies, scales automatically with usage, and benefits from Catalyst built-in authentication, audit
logging, and compliance features.
The build is planned as a 7-day sprint for a 2-3 person team, with all six capabilities from the problem statement
(Advanced Visualization, Network Link Analysis, Sociological and Predictive Dashboards, Pattern and Trend
Discovery, Network and Behavioral Analysis, AI/ML-Driven Intelligence) addressed in the MVP. The estimated
monthly cost for a 50-analyst pilot is under USD 200, well within the Catalyst credits provided to Datathon
participants. The platform is designed to be demo-ready: an SCRB analyst can sit down, drill into a district hotspot,
see the criminal network around a repeat offender, view the socio-economic correlates of an emerging trend, and
receive an AI forecast for the next 30 days, all in a single integrated dashboard with every visualization backed by a
full data lineage trail.

2. Problem Statement and Solution Approach
2.1 The Four Pain Points
The Karnataka State Police crime records ecosystem currently suffers from four interrelated pain points that the
Datathon challenge asks teams to solve. First, data silos and manual processes: crime records are managed in
independent silos at the police-station level, heavily reliant on Excel-based reporting rather than integrated,
automated systems. An analyst who wants to understand a cross-district trend must manually pull spreadsheets from
multiple stations, reconcile inconsistent formats, and perform analysis in a desktop tool that cannot scale. Second,
lack of advanced analytics: there is a notable absence of AI-driven approaches, leaving deeper behavioral
patterns, social interactions, and interconnected criminal networks undiscovered. The current tools can tell you what
happened; they cannot tell you why it happened, what is likely to happen next, or who is connected to whom across
cases.
Third, information gaps at the SCRB: the State Crime Records Bureau currently receives limited, fragmented
information from districts, hindering its ability to perform comprehensive state-wide analysis. The SCRB is the
body that should be the strategic intelligence hub for the state, but it is starved of the integrated data and analytical
tools it needs to play that role. Fourth, reactive versus proactive policing: without a systematic exploration of
emerging trends, investigators lack the tools for proactive policing strategies and evidence-based prevention. By the
time a trend is visible in monthly reports, the opportunity to deploy resources proactively has passed. The platform
must move the KSP from a posture of "what happened last month" to "what is emerging this week and what should
we do about it."
2.2 Our Solution Approach
We propose a platform with five design principles. First, integrated data layer: all crime records, offender and
victim data, MO taxonomy, socio-economic indicators, and financial transactions are consolidated into a single
Catalyst Data Store with consistent schema, eliminating silos at the data level. Second, three complementary
pipelines: rather than a single monolithic analytics engine, the platform runs three specialized pipelines
(visualization, network, predictive) that share the data layer and the explainability framework but operate
independently, so each can be built, tested, and demoed in isolation. Third, interactive and dynamic: every
visualization is interactive (drill-down, filter, hover, click), replacing static sheets with dynamic, spatial, and
relational storytelling as the problem statement demands. Fourth, AI-driven but explainable: every hotspot,
prediction, and anomaly carries a full data lineage trail showing source FIRs, feature pipeline version, model
version, confidence interval, and alternative hypotheses, so that AI is an accountable assistant rather than a black
box. Fifth, Catalyst-native: every component runs on Zoho Catalyst services, eliminating external dependencies
and keeping the system scalable, secure, and compliant.

3. Solution Architecture Overview
The KSP Crime Analytics and Visualization Platform is organized as a five-layer architecture. Each layer has a
clear responsibility and communicates only with the layers immediately above and below it, with cross-cutting
concerns (explainability, data lineage, multi-source integration, governance) woven through all layers. This
separation makes the system easy to reason about, easy to extend with new analytical capabilities, and easy to
deploy because each layer maps to a specific set of Catalyst services.
Figure 3.1 — Five-layer system architecture showing users, access, three analytics pipelines, Catalyst services, and cross-cutting
capabilities.
3.1 Layer Responsibilities
Layer 1 (Users and Visualization Channels) hosts the four user roles (Field Investigator, SCRB Analyst, District
SP/DCP, IGP/DGP Office) and the four interaction channels: the interactive web dashboard built in React with
D3.js and Mapbox for geospatial rendering, the geospatial map layer supporting district and police-station
drill-down with hotspot pulsing, the PDF intelligence report generator using SmartBrowz for scheduled and
on-demand briefings, and the real-time alert feed pushing red-zone notifications to field officers. Each role sees a
different slice of the data and a different default dashboard layout, enforced at Layer 2.
Layer 2 (API Gateway, Security, and RBAC) is the gatekeeper. The Catalyst API Gateway handles routing, JWT
authentication, rate limiting, and query throttling per role. Catalyst Authentication manages login and the four-role

hierarchy. Every dashboard view, every export, and every AI prediction is logged to an immutable audit trail with
an input data hash for reproducibility. The DPDP compliance module enforces PII masking, retention policy, and
data subject access requests. Row-level security ensures that a field investigator sees only their jurisdiction while
the SCRB analyst sees the full state.
Layer 3 (AI/ML Analytics Engine) is the brain of the platform. The Analytics Orchestrator receives every analyst
query, routes it to the appropriate pipeline, manages cache, schedules nightly batch jobs (hotspot recompute,
forecast refresh, anomaly scan), and streams results to dashboards in real time. The three pipelines are: the
Visualization and Spatial Pipeline (interactive dashboards, drill-down, KDE hotspot, emerging-trend pulsing,
seasonal decomposition); the Network Link Analysis Pipeline (suspect-victim-location graph, repeat-offender
tracking, MO clustering, association detection via community algorithms); and the Predictive and Anomaly Pipeline
(socio-economic correlation overlays, AI risk scoring, time-series forecasting, anomaly detection). Each pipeline
exposes a defined set of tools (Catalyst Functions) and produces explainable outputs with full data lineage.
Layer 4 (Catalyst Services) is the substrate. Serverless Functions host the analytics logic and tool
implementations. Zia AutoML trains and serves the forecasting and risk-scoring models. QuickML serves custom
ML models, embeddings for MO clustering, and feature engineering pipelines. Data Store holds the relational crime
database. NoSQL holds the network graph cache, anomaly log, and audit trail. Stratus stores dashboard snapshots
and PDF reports. Cache holds hot query results and pre-computed clusters. Slate hosts the React dashboard.
SmartBrowz renders PDF reports. Cron runs nightly batch jobs. Signals fires events when anomalies are detected,
triggering Push Notifications. Circuits orchestrates multi-step investigation workflows. Zia Services provides OCR
for scanned FIRs and translation. Mail sends scheduled intelligence briefings.
Layer 5 (Cross-cutting Capabilities) includes the Explainable Analytics framework (which attaches data lineage
and model cards to every dashboard cell and every prediction), the Data Lineage tracker (which traces every
analytical output back to its source FIRs through the ETL and feature pipeline), the Multi-source Integration layer
(which joins crime data with socio-economic indicators, urbanization patterns, and population data at district and
PS granularity), and the Governance and Audit subsystem (immutable access log, DPDP Act 2023 compliance,
role-based field redaction, retention policy). These concerns are not standalone modules; they are invoked by every
pipeline at every layer.
3.2 Example Data Flow
Consider an SCRB analyst who opens the dashboard and drills into a spike in thefts in Bengaluru North over the last
30 days. The dashboard calls the Visualization Pipeline, which queries Data Store for theft FIRs in Bengaluru North
for the period, runs hotspot KDE on the lat-lng of incident locations, computes an emerging-trend score comparing
the last 30 days to the historical baseline, and returns the heatmap with red-zone pulsing on cells where the trend
score exceeds the threshold. The analyst clicks a red-zone cell, which triggers the Network Pipeline to build a graph
of accused persons linked to FIRs in that cell, runs community detection, and overlays the network on the map. The
analyst then clicks "Forecast next 30 days," which triggers the Predictive Pipeline to run the Zia AutoML
forecasting model for theft in Bengaluru North, returning a forecast with confidence intervals and a
feature-importance breakdown showing which socio-economic indicators are driving the forecast. Every
visualization carries a data lineage trail, and the analyst can right-click any element to see the source FIRs and the
model version. The full session is logged to the audit trail.

4. Analytics Pipeline Flow
The analytics pipeline flow describes how raw crime data is transformed into actionable intelligence delivered
through the dashboard. The flow has seven stages, with three parallel pipelines at Stage 4 and a feedback loop from
Stage 7 back to the models. This design ensures that the platform is not a one-shot batch system but a continuously
improving intelligence hub where analyst feedback refines the AI models over time.
Figure 4.1 — Analytics pipeline flow showing 7 stages from data ingestion through 3 parallel pipelines to dashboard delivery with
feedback loop.
4.1 Stage 1: Multi-Source Data Ingestion

4.2 Stage 2: ETL and Feature Engineering
The ETL stage runs as a nightly Cron job and on-demand for real-time data. It performs deduplication (matching

4.3 Stage 3: Analytics Orchestrator
The Analytics Orchestrator is the coordinator. When an analyst opens the dashboard or runs a query, the
Orchestrator receives the request, determines which pipeline(s) can satisfy it, checks the cache for pre-computed
results, dispatches to the pipeline(s) if cache is cold, collects the results, attaches explainability metadata, persists to
NoSQL for audit, and streams to the dashboard. The Orchestrator also manages the nightly batch schedule: hotspot
recompute at 2 AM, forecast refresh at 3 AM, anomaly scan at 4 AM, and graph community refresh at 5 AM. It
manages cache TTLs (24 hours for hotspots, 7 days for communities, 1 hour for trend queries) and invalidates
cache when new data is ingested.
4.4 Stage 4: Three Parallel Pipelines
The three pipelines run in parallel where the query requires multiple capabilities. The Visualization Pipeline
exposes tools: trend_aggregate() for time-series aggregation, hotspot_kde() for kernel-density-estimation hotspot

tools: build_graph(seed) for constructing a subgraph around an entity, detect_communities() for Louvain
community detection, repeat_offender_profile() for aggregating an offender's history across jurisdictions,
mo_cluster() for clustering modus operandi via embeddings, and centrality() for betweenness and degree centrality.

risk_score(district) for AI-driven district risk scoring, forecast(category) for time-series forecasting,

5.2 Key Relationships and Multi-Source Joins
The schema captures the essential entities of a crime database and the relationships between them, with explicit
support for the multi-source joins that the Predictive Pipeline requires. An FIR belongs to one police station and
has one crime type; it links to multiple accused (via fir_accused), multiple victims (via fir_victim), and multiple
MO tags (via fir_mo). Each accused has an offender history (prior FIRs linked via offender_history) which is the
basis for repeat-offender identification. Financial transactions link to accused persons, enabling money-trail
analysis. The socio_economic_indicator table is at district-year granularity and joins to FIRs via the police_station
district, enabling the Predictive Pipeline to correlate crime patterns with literacy, unemployment, migration,
urbanization, and GDP per capita. This relational structure supports all three pipelines: the Visualization Pipeline
aggregates FIR by date and location, the Network Pipeline traverses fir_accused and offender_history to build
co-accused graphs, and the Predictive Pipeline joins FIR aggregations to socio_economic_indicator for correlation
and forecasting.

6.1 Why Catalyst-Native Wins
Building entirely on Catalyst delivers four concrete advantages for a law enforcement analytics platform. First,
compliance boundary: all data, all logs, and all AI model training stay within the Catalyst trust boundary, which
simplifies DPDP Act 2023 compliance and any future law enforcement certification. The SCRB can be confident
that no crime data ever leaves the Catalyst environment. Second, cost predictability: Catalyst serverless pricing
means the platform costs near zero when idle and scales linearly with usage, with the Catalyst credits provided to
Datathon participants covering the entire prototype phase and the first month of pilot. Third, operational
simplicity: there is no Kubernetes cluster to manage, no GPU to provision, no model server to babysit, and no
separate authentication system to integrate, which is critical for a small team in a one-week sprint. Fourth, native
integration: Catalyst services are designed to work together, so Functions can invoke Zia AutoML with a single

Functions and Push Notifications without any message bus configuration. This native integration is what allows the

7. Visualization and Spatial Pipeline (Capabilities 1
& 4)
The Visualization Pipeline is the most visible part of the platform and the primary way most users interact with the
analytics. It replaces the static Excel sheets and PDF reports that currently dominate KSP analytics with interactive
dashboards and geospatial maps that support drill-down, filtering, and dynamic storytelling. The pipeline is built on
ECharts for chart rendering, Mapbox for geospatial rendering, and a set of Catalyst Functions that implement the
analytical tools. Every visualization is role-aware (an investigator sees their jurisdiction, an SCRB analyst sees the
full state) and every visualization carries a data lineage trail.
7.1 District-Level Drill-Down
The drill-down capability is the core navigation paradigm of the dashboard. An analyst lands on the state view,
which shows Karnataka with crime density by district. Clicking a district zooms in to show the police stations
within that district, with crime density by PS. Clicking a PS zooms in further to show the beats within that PS, with
crime density by beat. At each level, the analyst can switch between crime types, time ranges, and visualization
modes (heatmap, choropleth, bubble chart). The drill-down is implemented via the drill_down(level, parent_id)
Function, which queries Data Store with the appropriate aggregation level and returns GeoJSON-compatible shapes
for Mapbox rendering. The drill-down state is preserved in the URL so analysts can bookmark specific views and
share them with colleagues.
7.2 Spatiotemporal Hotspot Detection
Hotspot detection uses kernel density estimation (KDE) on the latitude-longitude of incident locations, with time as
a third dimension. The hotspot_kde(district, crime_type, date_range) Function divides the selected geography into a
grid (500m x 500m for urban areas, 2km x 2km for rural), assigns each FIR in the date range to a cell, and
computes a density score per cell as the sum of Gaussian kernels centered on each incident, weighted by crime
severity. The result is a heatmap overlay rendered on Mapbox. The computation runs nightly via Cron and is cached
in Catalyst Cache for 24 hours, so the dashboard loads instantly. When an analyst filters by crime type or date
range, the cached grid is filtered client-side, providing interactive exploration without re-computing. For the
prototype, the grid covers all 31 districts of Karnataka with three years of synthetic data, producing a visually
compelling heatmap that demonstrates the capability clearly.
7.3 Emerging Trend Alerts and Red-Zone Pulsing
The emerging trend alert capability is what moves the platform from reactive to proactive. The
emerging_trend_score(district, crime_type, window_days=30) Function compares the recent crime count (last 30
days) to the historical baseline (same period in previous years, adjusted for seasonality) and computes a z-score. If
the z-score exceeds a configurable threshold (default 2.0, meaning the recent count is more than two standard
deviations above the baseline), the cell is flagged as an emerging trend and written to the emerging_trends NoSQL
collection. On the dashboard, flagged cells are rendered with a red-zone pulsing animation that draws the analyst's
attention. The alert is also pushed via Catalyst Signals to Push Notifications, so field officers and SPs receive a
real-time alert on their phones when a red-zone triggers. The alert includes the district, crime type, the current
count, the baseline, the z-score, and a link to the dashboard view. This is the single most demo-worthy feature of the

7.4 Seasonal and Temporal Decomposition
The seasonal_decomp(district, crime_type, window_months=36) Function applies the classical STL
(Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess) method to separate a crime time series into three components: the
long-term trend, the seasonal pattern (e.g., thefts peaking during Diwali, chain-snatching in summer evenings), and
the residual. This decomposition helps policymakers distinguish between a genuine upward trend in crime (which
may require policy intervention such as increased patrols or social programs) and a seasonal spike (which may
require temporary deployment of additional resources). The decomposition is rendered as a four-panel chart: the
original series, the trend component, the seasonal component, and the residual. The analyst can hover over any point
to see the exact values and the contributing FIRs. For the prototype, the synthetic data includes programmed
seasonal patterns so the demo clearly shows the decomposition working.

specific filter combination and bookmark it for daily monitoring.

The Network Pipeline exposes five tools. build_graph(seed_entity, depth=2) constructs a subgraph around a seed
entity (an accused, a victim, a location, or an incident), querying Data Store for all directly connected entities (depth
1) and then for all entities connected to those (depth 2), assembling the subgraph in memory. detect_communities()
runs the Louvain community detection algorithm on the constructed graph, partitioning it into communities that
often correspond to organized crime groups. repeat_offender_profile(accused_id) aggregates an offender's history
across jurisdictions, producing a structured profile with their prior FIRs, co-accused network, MO evolution, and
geographic spread, with a repeat-offender flag if they have 3+ prior cases. mo_cluster(crime_type) clusters the
modus operandi descriptions of all FIRs of a given crime type using QuickML embeddings and K-means, surfacing
clusters of similar MOs that may indicate a serial perpetrator. centrality() computes betweenness and degree
centrality on the graph, identifying key actors who sit on many shortest paths (often brokers, financiers, or
coordinators) and the most active offenders (highest degree).
8.3 Visualization and Interaction
The graph is visualized using ECharts force-directed layout, producing an interactive node-link diagram. Nodes are
color-coded by type (accused, victim, location, account, incident) and sized by centrality score, so the most
important entities are immediately visible. Edges are color-coded by type (co-accused, financial, MO similarity)
and thickness-weighted by edge weight. The user can click any node to see a detail panel with the entity attributes
and a mini-list of its connections. The "Find Communities" button runs Louvain and overlays community
boundaries as translucent hulls. The "Find Similar MOs" button runs mo_cluster and highlights MO-similarity
edges. The visualization supports search: typing an accused name highlights that node and its immediate
neighborhood. This interactivity is critical for investigative use, where an analyst needs to explore the network
fluidly rather than viewing a static image.
8.4 Repeat Offender Tracking Across Jurisdictions
A key problem statement requirement is "repeat offender tracking" that "links an individual to multiple incidents,
highlighting their specific Modus Operandi (MO) across different jurisdictions." The repeat_offender_profile tool
does exactly this: it queries the offender_history table for all prior FIRs involving the accused (matching by name
and DOB, with alias resolution), aggregates them, and produces a profile showing the offender's MO evolution over
time, their geographic spread across districts, their co-offender network, and their conviction status. The profile is
rendered as a dedicated panel in the dashboard with a timeline of incidents, a map of locations, a mini-graph of
co-offenders, and a list of MO clusters the offender belongs to. This is the kind of cross-jurisdictional intelligence
that is impossible to assemble from isolated Excel sheets and that the SCRB currently lacks.

9. Predictive and Anomaly Pipeline (Capabilities 3 &
6)
The Predictive Pipeline moves the SCRB from reactive reporting to a "Strategic Intelligence Hub," as the problem
statement puts it. It overlays crime data with socio-economic indicators to understand the "why" behind the
"where," deploys AI-driven risk scoring on districts to forecast potential high-risk areas, runs time-series
forecasting to predict emerging crime typologies, and performs anomaly detection to flag incidents that deviate
from standard behavioral patterns. The pipeline is built on Zia AutoML for automated model training and
QuickML for custom feature engineering, with every prediction backed by a feature-importance breakdown for
explainability.
9.1 Socio-Economic Correlation
The socio_correlation(indicator, crime_type) Function joins FIR aggregations (by district and year) to the
socio_economic_indicator table and computes the correlation between a socio-economic indicator (literacy rate,
unemployment rate, migration index, urbanization rate, GDP per capita) and a crime type (theft, assault,
cybercrime, etc.) across districts and over time. The correlation is computed as a Pearson coefficient with a p-value,
and the result is rendered as a scatter plot with a regression line and a correlation matrix heatmap showing all
indicator-crime-type pairs. The analyst can drill into any cell of the matrix to see the underlying scatter plot and the
contributing district-level data points. This is the "sociological insight" capability from the problem statement,
providing the "why" behind the "where" of crime patterns. For the prototype, the synthetic data includes
programmed correlations (e.g., theft correlated with unemployment, cybercrime correlated with urbanization) so
the demo clearly shows meaningful correlations.
9.2 AI-Driven Predictive Risk Scoring
The risk_score(district, crime_type, horizon_days=30) Function uses a Zia AutoML-trained model to forecast the
risk of a specific crime type in a specific district over the next 30 days. The model is trained on historical FIR data
(3 years), socio-economic indicators, seasonal features (day-of-week, month, festival periods), and lag features
(crime count in the previous 7, 14, and 30 days). The output is a risk score from 0 to 100 and a confidence interval.
The risk scores are rendered as a choropleth map of Karnataka, with districts colored from green (low risk) to red
(high risk). The analyst can click any district to see the feature-importance breakdown, showing which features are
driving the risk score (e.g., "unemployment rate increased 12%, recent theft count up 18%, festival period
approaching"). This explainability is critical for investigator trust and for legal defensibility: an SP who deploys
resources based on a risk score needs to be able to justify the deployment.
The model is retrained weekly via a Cron job using the latest data, and the model version is recorded with every
prediction so that prior predictions can be reproduced. The anomaly_log collection captures analyst feedback on
predictions (was the forecast accurate? did the predicted spike materialize?), and this feedback is used to adjust the
model retraining. This is the feedback loop shown in Figure 4.1, and it is what makes the platform a continuously
improving intelligence hub rather than a one-shot deployment.

The forecast(district, crime_type, horizon_days=30) Function produces a detailed time-series forecast using a Zia
AutoML model. Unlike the risk_score Function (which produces a single number), the forecast Function produces
a full time series: predicted crime count for each of the next 30 days, with a 95% confidence interval. The forecast
is rendered as a line chart with the historical data, the forecast, and the confidence interval shaded. The analyst can
switch between districts and crime types, and can adjust the forecast horizon. The forecast is refreshed nightly by
the Cron job and cached in the forecast_cache NoSQL collection for fast dashboard access. For the prototype, the
forecasting model is a gradient-boosted regressor trained on 3 years of synthetic data with programmed trend and
seasonal components, so the demo clearly shows the model capturing both the upward trend and the seasonal
patterns.
9.4 Anomaly Detection
The anomaly_detect(district, crime_type, window_days=7) Function flags incidents that deviate from standard
behavioral patterns. It uses an isolation forest model (trained via Zia AutoML on historical data) that considers
multiple features simultaneously: crime count, crime type mix, geographic spread, time-of-day distribution, and
MO distribution. When the model detects a statistically significant deviation (an anomaly score above a threshold),
it writes an anomaly record to the anomaly_log collection with the detected features, the source FIRs, the model
version, and a confidence score. The anomaly is rendered on the dashboard as a callout with a "Why is this
anomalous?" drill-down that shows which features deviated from the norm. The analyst can confirm or reject the
anomaly, and the feedback is used to refine the model. This is the "anomaly detection for incidents that deviate
from standard behavioral patterns" capability from the problem statement, "assisting investigators in linking
complex cases" that might otherwise go unnoticed.

10. Explainable AI and Data Lineage
Explainability is not a feature layered on top of the KSP Crime Analytics Platform; it is a structural property of
how every pipeline operates. Law enforcement AI systems face a higher bar of accountability than typical
consumer AI: an SP who deploys resources based on a risk score may be called to justify that deployment, and a
complex case linked by anomaly detection may need to be defended in court. Our framework addresses this by
making every analytical output, every dashboard cell, and every AI prediction traceable end-to-end through a
structured data lineage trail.

10.3 Compliance, Reproducibility, and Feedback
The data lineage framework directly supports three compliance and operational requirements. First,
reproducibility: given the same data state, the same ETL version, and the same model version, the same analytical
output would be produced, which is critical for legal defensibility. The lineage document records all three, so any
output can be reproduced months or years later. Second, accountability: every dashboard view and every
prediction is linked to an audit_trail_id that captures who viewed it, when, from what IP, with what role, and what
RBAC decision was made. Third, continuous improvement: the anomaly_log and forecast_cache collections
capture analyst feedback (confirmed/rejected, accurate/inaccurate), and the weekly Zia AutoML retraining job
uses this feedback to adjust model thresholds and improve precision. This feedback loop is what transforms the
platform from a static deployment into a continuously improving intelligence hub that gets better the more analysts
use it.

11. Security, RBAC, and Governance
A crime analytics platform handling sensitive data about accused persons, victims, and ongoing investigations
requires a security and governance posture that goes beyond typical web application security. The platform enforces
role-based access control at the API layer, row-level security at the database layer, immutable audit logging at the
application layer, and DPDP Act 2023 compliance at the data-retention layer. Every component is implemented
with Catalyst native services, so there is no separate identity provider or audit system to integrate.

11.2 PII Protection and DPDP Compliance
Personally identifiable information (victim names, addresses, contact details, accused family information) is
masked at the application layer based on the user role. Investigators see full PII for cases in their jurisdiction but
masked PII for cases outside their jurisdiction that appear in network or pattern results. SCRB analysts see masked
PII by default with an "unmask" action that is itself audited. The IGP/DGP office sees no PII at all, only aggregate
numbers. Data retention follows DPDP Act 2023 guidelines: FIR data is retained for 10 years, audit logs for 7
years, anomaly logs for 2 years, after which automated Cron jobs purge the data and log the purge action. Data
subject access requests (DSARs) under DPDP can be fulfilled by querying the audit log and lineage trails for a
given individual. The Catalyst-native deployment ensures all data stays within the Catalyst trust boundary,
simplifying compliance.

12. Implementation Timeline — 7-Day Sprint
The build is planned as a 7-day sprint for a 2-3 person team. The plan front-loads the foundational pieces (schema,

12.1 Critical Path and Risk
The critical path runs through Day 1 (foundation) to Day 2 (orchestrator + visualization) to Day 5 (predictive
pipeline), because the predictive pipeline depends on the Zia AutoML model which depends on the ETL feature
engineering which depends on the schema. If the foundation slips by a day, the entire timeline slips. To mitigate
this, Day 1 is scoped conservatively: the schema is minimal (14 tables), the synthetic data generator is simple
(Python with faker), and RBAC is role-based only. The three pipelines on Days 2-6 can be built in parallel because
they share the same orchestrator contract, so if one pipeline is delayed, the others are not blocked. The biggest risk
is Zia AutoML model training time on Day 5; to mitigate, the model is trained on a small subset first (one district,
one crime type) to validate the pipeline, then scaled to full state overnight.

15.1 Testing Strategy
The testing strategy has three layers. Unit tests cover each pipeline tool function in isolation, using mock Data
Store responses, and validate that the tool produces correct outputs and correct lineage trails for known inputs.
Integration tests cover the full flow from API Gateway through Orchestrator through pipeline and back, using a
test Data Store with known data, and validate that the lineage trail is complete and that RBAC is enforced.
Evaluation tests use a curated set of 50 sample queries (15 visualization, 15 network, 15 predictive, 5 compound)
with expected response shapes and expected lineage contents; these are run before every demo and before every
deployment to catch regressions. The eval set is version-controlled alongside the code, and any change to pipeline
tools or model versions must pass the eval set before merge. For the predictive pipeline, the eval set also includes
accuracy benchmarks: the forecast model must achieve a mean absolute percentage error below 25% on a 6-month
holdout, and the anomaly detection model must achieve a precision above 0.7 on expert-labeled samples.

16. Future Roadmap and Scaling
The hackathon prototype is the foundation for a production crime analytics platform. The roadmap below outlines
the evolution over 6 and 12 months, assuming the prototype wins or secures pilot funding. The roadmap prioritizes
real data integration (the single highest-value next step), then scaling, then ecosystem integration with national
systems.
16.1 Six-Month Milestones
Month 1-2: Production Data Integration. Work with KSP to connect the platform to a production copy of the
crime database (initially read-only), validate that all three pipelines work on real data, and run a pilot with one
district (Bengaluru Urban). This is the highest-risk phase because real data is messier than synthetic data: missing
fields, inconsistent date formats, duplicate records, and varying data entry quality across stations. The ETL pipeline
includes validation, deduplication, and enrichment steps. The ETL is also where the platform adds the most value
initially: cleaning and standardizing the data so that downstream analytics is possible.
Month 3-4: Scale to 5 Districts and Mobile App. Roll out to 5 districts (Bengaluru Urban, Bengaluru Rural,
Mysuru, Hubli-Dharwad, Mangaluru), train SCRB analysts and district SPs on the platform, and build a mobile app
for field investigators using the same Catalyst backend. The mobile app gives field officers access to hotspots,
repeat-offender profiles, and red-zone alerts on their phones, closing the loop between strategic intelligence and
tactical action. The mobile app is built in React Native and uses the same API Gateway as the web dashboard.
Month 5-6: Advanced AI and Feedback Loop. Enhance the predictive pipeline with deep-learning models for
anomaly detection (replacing isolation forest with autoencoders), add a "What-if Analysis" tool that lets an SP
simulate the effect of deploying resources to a district, and mature the feedback loop so that analyst feedback
drives weekly model retraining automatically. By month 6, the platform should be running in 5 districts with 200+
active users, handling 10,000+ queries per day, and demonstrably improving forecast accuracy and anomaly
precision through the feedback loop.
16.2 Twelve-Month Vision
Month 7-12: State-wide Rollout and Ecosystem Integration. Roll out to all 31 districts of Karnataka in phases
(5 districts per month), with training and change management. Integrate with CCTNS (Crime and Criminal
Tracking Network and Systems) and ICJS (Interoperable Criminal Justice System) via Catalyst Connections OAuth,
so that the platform becomes the analytical layer on top of the existing national systems. Explore federated learning
across districts to improve forecasting without centralizing raw data. Add a "Strategic Intelligence Hub" view for
the IGP/DGP office that synthesizes all three pipelines into a single executive dashboard with policy
recommendations. By month 12, the platform should be the primary analytical tool for KSP, handling thousands of
queries per day, surfacing insights that would otherwise remain hidden, and moving the KSP from reactive
reporting to proactive, evidence-based policing across the state.
